⭐ The only 8 things to memorise
Learn these cold. Everything below is recognition-only.
1You design a system, not prices. At tens of millions of listings, decide which items get which logic, what the algorithm optimizes, what bounds it, and how you know it works.
2Triage by impact into tiers: price-image items (KVIs) get tight, competitor-anchored control; the mid-tail gets algorithmic pricing within guardrails; the long tail and new items get rules or pooled estimates.
3State the objective before the method. Maximize contribution profit subject to constraints (price index on KVIs, GMV floor, sell-through). "Traffic" and "margin" are different objectives with different tools.
4The price the customer sees is the effective price after coupons, instant discounts, card offers and WOW pricing. Never analyze list price alone.
5Margin means contribution margin (net of fulfillment, delivery, promo funding), not gross margin. Cost-to-serve varies by item.
6Observational elasticity is biased. Promos coincide with demand spikes and competitors react to demand. Use experiments or quasi-random variation, and pool estimates (category → brand → SKU) for sparse data.
7Evaluate at category or basket level, not per item: cannibalization, substitution to 3P, and halo effects all hide in SKU-level reads. Randomize by time, geo or cluster, not SKU.
8Every answer: Objective → Segment → Lever → Evidence → Guardrails → Measurement, then close with a recommendation, the risk, and how you'd roll it out in waves.
Two habits that carry every answer: quantify scale ("~2K KVIs drive X% of traffic; the long tail runs on rules") · name what you would not do and why.
🧭 The core shift: system, not prices
The frame that makes every other answer coherent.
Say this"At this scale nobody decides individual prices, so I think in tiers. A few thousand price-image items drive perception and traffic, so those get competitor-anchored control and close monitoring. The mid-tail runs on an algorithm optimizing contribution profit inside guardrails. The long tail and new items run on rules or pooled estimates, because per-SKU modeling there is noise. My job is deciding which tier an item sits in, what objective it serves, and how we measure it."
TierItemsApproach
Price-image items (KVIs)Key value items: the few staples customers remember and compare, so they shape the "cheap" perception (diapers, water, eggs, rice). A few thousand SKUs, picked by traffic, frequency, price visibility and sensitivity to gaps. The list shifts with seasonality and inflation, so it needs an owner.Competitor-anchored price index targets, often semi-manual, monitored in near-real time. Margin is the constraint, not the objective. Don't auto-match: hold if delivery or WOW value carries the item.
Mid-tailLarge, steady-selling catalog with enough data for elasticity.Algorithmic pricing maximizing contribution profit within guardrails. Pooled elasticity estimates, tested via experiments.
Long tail / new itemsSparse or no history.Rules (cost-plus, category benchmark), fast learning, pooled priors from the category. Don't build per-SKU models.
If probed → how items move between tiers (traffic, velocity, data volume) · who owns the KVI list · how often tiers are re-evaluated · what happens when a long-tail item suddenly goes viral.
🛒 Coupang context
Inferred from public information. Frame as "my understanding is… correct me" in the interview.
1P (Rocket)Coupang owns inventory and sets the price, so it owns the margin. Levers: price, promo funding, assortment. Objective can be directly optimized.
3P (Marketplace)Sellers set prices. Coupang influences through fees, promotions, ranking and Buy Box-style placement, plus Rocket Fulfillment for sellers. Levers are indirect, so the objectives differ: price competitiveness and conversion for the platform, not unit margin.
WOW membershipFree fast delivery and member pricing are bundled value. Over-discounting can cannibalize membership value, and member-only prices need their own measurement.
Price comparisonKorean shoppers compare heavily (for example via Naver Shopping), so competitive price position drives traffic share. Competitor price tracking is a core input.
Logistics costDelivery and fulfillment cost vary by size, weight and temperature (fresh versus ambient). Use contribution margin, not gross.
Promotion stackingCoupons, instant discounts, card discounts and bundles interact. Analyze the effective price and who funds each piece (Coupang, seller, card issuer).
SpeedCompetitors move prices constantly, so you need near-real-time reaction, automated alerts, and clear human ownership of exceptions.
Non-price valueDelivery speed is a competitive advantage. For some items the right answer is not to match a lower competitor price, because the delivery promise carries the value.
⚙️ Components of a pricing system
1. ObjectiveMaximize contribution profit subject to constraints: price-index targets on KVIs, a GMV floor, sell-through for aging inventory. State this first, always.
2. InputsCost and cost-to-serve, competitor prices, demand forecast, elasticity estimates, inventory and age, promo calendar, seasonality.
3. Decision logicRules, an optimization model, or a hybrid. Rules for cold-start and KVIs; a model for the mid-tail. Keep logic explainable so category managers trust it.
4. GuardrailsMargin floors, max price change per day, parity limits, brand or legal constraints, circuit breakers for competitor-triggered price wars.
5. MeasurementA/B or switchback tests with a permanent holdout, and a metric tree: contribution profit per order, price index, GMV, conversion.
6. Monitoring & overrideAnomaly alerts (price spikes, margin breaches, stale data), exception ownership, an audit trail of why a price was set, and a feedback loop into the model.
⚠️ Failure modeA "reasonable" algorithm quietly chasing a bad competitor price feed or a stale cost. Data-quality checks on inputs matter as much as the model.
🔬 Hard problems at scale
Where seniority shows. Pick two or three per answer, don't recite all.
Say this"Historical price changes weren't random: promos coincide with demand spikes and competitors react to demand, so a naive log-log regression overstates how price-insensitive customers are. I'd use experiments where I can, quasi-random variation where I can't, and pool across the category so sparse SKUs borrow strength."
Sparse dataHierarchical or pooled elasticity (category → brand → SKU) with shrinkage toward the parent. Don't regress per-SKU on a few noisy points.
EndogeneityPrice correlates with unobserved demand shocks. Fix with randomized price tests, instruments (supplier cost shocks), or event-based variation. Say "biased toward zero or wrong sign" and explain why.
Cross-effectsCannibalization within category, substitution to 3P, basket halo (cheap diapers driving wipes). Evaluate at the category or basket level.
Experiment interferenceSKU-level randomization leaks across substitutes and shared inventory. Use switchback (by time), geo, or cluster-level randomization. Check for novelty effects.
Short vs. long runDiscounts pull demand forward and shape reference prices. Look at post-promo dips, repeat rate, and WOW retention, not just the promo week.
Competitor reactionPrice wars. Model best-response and set guardrails; don't assume competitors hold still.
Metric choiceContribution profit per order, price index versus competitors, GMV, conversion, margin dollars. Revenue alone rewards bad discounting.
Automation vs. manualAutomate where rules are stable and volume is high; keep humans on KVIs, new categories and exceptions. Define the handoff explicitly.
🗣️ Articulating a pricing decision
Lead with the named structure. Spell it out, don't abbreviate it.
StructureObjective → Segment → Lever → Evidence → Guardrails → Measurement

Worked example: "How would you decide whether to cut prices on category X?"

ObjectiveIs the goal traffic and price perception, or margin? That decides everything. Assume we want share without destroying contribution profit.
SegmentDon't treat the category as one unit. Split into price-sensitive, high-traffic items where we're above competitors, and the long tail where cuts barely move volume.
LeverA permanent cut, targeted coupon, member-only price, or bundle? Each differs in cost and reversibility.
EvidenceElasticity by segment, competitor gaps, cross-effect on substitutes. For a decision this size rely on a test, since observational elasticities are biased by promo timing.
GuardrailsMargin floor per item, spend cap, automatic rollback if contribution profit per order falls below a threshold.
MeasurementGeo or switchback test with a holdout, reading contribution profit, traffic and conversion, plus the post-period for pull-forward.
CloseScale in waves: segments with proven lift first, automate once rules are stable. Name the main risk (competitor price war) and the cap that contains it.

📊 Metrics & formulas
Contribution profitNet revenue − COGS − fulfillment/delivery − promo funding borne by Coupang. The objective for most mid-tail pricing.
Price indexWeighted average of (our price ÷ competitor price) across a basket, weighted by traffic or sales. 100 = parity. Track separately for KVIs and overall.
Own-price elasticityε = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP. In a log-log model ln Q = α + ε ln P + controls, the coefficient is ε. |ε| > 1 means cutting price raises revenue; margin needs a separate check.
Cross-elasticity%ΔQ of item B for a 1% price change in item A. Negative for complements (diapers → wipes), positive for substitutes.
Break-even liftFor a price cut of d on margin m: required volume lift = d ÷ (m − d). A 5-point cut on 20% margin needs a 33% volume lift to break even on unit profit.
Incrementality(Treatment outcome − control outcome) ÷ incremental cost. Distinguishes promo-driven demand from demand that would have happened anyway.
Pull-forwardPost-promo dip in baseline sales. Net lift = promo-period lift − post-period dip.
Report quality checksFreshness, row-count and null checks, reconciliation against finance, definition ownership, metric versioning. "Same number, one definition."
🧰 Tooling refresher
Expert SQL is a basic qualification. Know the patterns and the vocabulary, don't over-invest.
Price index by daySUM(our_price/comp_price * weight) / SUM(weight) grouped by date and category; join our price table to the competitor feed on item match key and date. Watch the item-matching quality.
Price change detectionprice - LAG(price) OVER (PARTITION BY sku ORDER BY date); flag changes above a threshold for the anomaly report.
Promo vs. baselineBaseline = trailing median of non-promo days; lift = promo-day units ÷ baseline. Exclude stockout days.
Stockout biasZero sales on a stockout day isn't zero demand. Filter or impute before estimating elasticity.
Spark / HadoopDistributed processing for large item × day × competitor tables. Know partitioning, broadcast joins and avoiding skew on top SKUs.
AirflowOrchestrates the daily pipeline: competitor feed → cleaning → price index → report. Know retries, dependencies, backfills, SLAs and data-quality gates.
GitHubVersion-controlled SQL and model code, code review, and documented metric definitions.
